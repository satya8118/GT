Topic 1

Definition & Intuition of Planar Graphs

In graph theory, an abstract graph $G = (V, E)$ is just a set of vertices and a set of edges connecting them—how we draw it on paper usually doesn't change the graph itself. However, in many real-world problems (such as designing printed circuit boards, laying out subway lines, or drawing maps), we need to know whether a network can be drawn on a flat surface without any edges crossing each other.

Formal Definition (Planar Graph): A graph $G = (V, E)$ is called planar if it can be drawn in the two-dimensional plane ($\mathbb{R}^2$) in such a way that no two edges intersect anywhere except at a common endpoint (vertex) where they meet.

Plane Graph (Planar Embedding): Once we actually draw a planar graph on a flat plane with zero edge crossings, that specific crossing-free drawing is called a planar embedding or a plane graph.

Crucial Distinction: Crossing Drawing vs. Non-Planar Graph

A very common mistake is looking at a drawing with crossed lines and immediately concluding the graph is non-planar. Remember: a graph is planar if there exists at least one way to draw it without crossings.

  • Example — The Complete Graph $K_4$: $K_4$ has 4 vertices where every vertex is connected to the other 3 ($E = 6$ edges). If you draw the 4 vertices as the corners of a square and draw the 2 diagonals straight across the middle, the two diagonals cross in the center.
  • How to Untangle $K_4$: That crossing is only a flaw of our drawing! We can keep the 4 vertices in a square, keep one diagonal inside the square, and simply curve the second diagonal around the outside of the square. Or, equivalently, we can place 3 vertices in a triangle and put the 4th vertex right in the center. Now there are 0 crossings, proving that $K_4$ is planar.
Topic 2a & 2b

Proving Trees, Cycle Graphs ($C_n$), and Wheel Graphs ($W_n$) are Planar

2a. Proof that Every Tree is Planar

A tree $T$ is a connected graph that contains no cycles. Every tree with $V \ge 1$ vertices has exactly $E = V - 1$ edges, and every tree with $V \ge 2$ vertices has at least two leaves (vertices of degree 1). We can prove that every tree is planar using mathematical induction on the number of vertices $V$:

  1. Base Case ($V = 1$): A tree with 1 vertex has $E = 0$ edges. A single point in the plane has no edges to cross, so it is trivially planar.
  2. Inductive Hypothesis: Assume that every tree with $k$ vertices ($k \ge 1$) can be drawn in the plane without any edge crossings.
  3. Inductive Step ($V = k + 1$): Let $T$ be a tree with $k + 1$ vertices. Since $k + 1 \ge 2$, $T$ must have a leaf vertex $v$ of degree 1, connected to a single parent vertex $u$ by a single edge $e = uv$.
  4. If we temporarily remove $v$ and the edge $uv$, the remaining graph $T' = T - v$ is still connected and cycle-free, so $T'$ is a tree with $k$ vertices. By our Inductive Hypothesis, $T'$ has a crossing-free drawing in the plane.
  5. Now look at the vertex $u$ in that drawing of $T'$. Because $T'$ has only a finite number of vertices and edges, we can draw a tiny circle (an $\varepsilon$-neighborhood) around $u$ small enough that no other vertices or unrelated edges pass through it. We simply place the leaf $v$ inside this tiny circle and draw a short straight segment from $u$ to $v$. This adds 0 crossings! Thus, $T$ is planar. $\blacksquare$

2b. Proof that Cycle Graphs ($C_n$) and Wheel Graphs ($W_n$) are Planar

  • Cycle Graphs ($C_n$, for $n \ge 3$): A cycle graph $C_n$ consists of $n$ vertices connected in a single closed loop: $v_1 - v_2 - \dots - v_n - v_1$. We can always embed $C_n$ in the plane by placing the $n$ vertices in order along the boundary of a circle (forming a regular $n$-sided polygon) and connecting adjacent vertices along the perimeter. None of the perimeter edges intersect except at their shared corners, so every $C_n$ is planar.
  • Wheel Graphs ($W_n$): A wheel graph is built by taking a cycle $C_n$ (the outer rim) and adding one central "hub" vertex $v_0$ that connects to every vertex on the rim via "spoke" edges. To draw $W_n$ without crossings:
    1. First draw the cycle $C_n$ as a regular polygon centered at the origin $(0,0)$.
    2. Place the hub vertex $v_0$ strictly at the center $(0,0)$ inside the polygon.
    3. Draw straight line segments (spokes) radially outward from $(0,0)$ to each rim vertex $v_1, \dots, v_n$. Since each spoke travels at a distinct angle from the center, no two spokes can cross each other, and none cross the outer rim. Thus, every wheel graph is planar. $\blacksquare$
Topic 2c

$K_5$: Smallest Vertex Count

Claim: $K_5$ is the non-planar graph with the smallest number of vertices ($V = 5$).

Explanation & Verification:

  • What are the parameters of $K_5$? It has $V = 5$ vertices, and every vertex connects to the other 4, giving $E = \binom{5}{2} = \frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10$ edges.
  • Why can't any simple graph with $V \le 4$ be non-planar? Any simple graph with $1, 2, 3,$ or $4$ vertices is a subgraph of the complete graph $K_4$. In Topic 1, we already showed how to draw $K_4$ in the plane with zero crossings. Deleting vertices or edges from a planar drawing of $K_4$ can never create crossings!
  • Therefore, every simple graph with $V \le 4$ is planar. Since $K_5$ has $V = 5$ vertices and is non-planar (which we will prove both geometrically and via Euler's Corollary in Topic 3c), 5 is the minimum possible number of vertices for a non-planar graph.
  • Geometric intuition (Jordan Curve argument): In $K_5$, the 5 vertices form a cycle $v_1 - v_2 - v_3 - v_4 - v_5 - v_1$ (a pentagon). There are 5 remaining "chord" edges. Inside the pentagon, you can draw at most 2 chords (say $v_1 v_3$ and $v_1 v_4$) before any 3rd interior chord is forced to cross them. Similarly, outside the pentagon, you can route at most 2 chords ($v_2 v_4$ and $v_2 v_5$) without crossing. The 5th chord ($v_3 v_5$) is trapped with nowhere to go!
Topic 2d

$K_{3,3}$: Smallest Edge Count

Claim: $K_{3,3}$ is the non-planar graph with the smallest number of edges ($E = 9$).

Explanation & Verification:

  • $K_{3,3}$ (the famous "Three Utilities Graph") is a complete bipartite graph with two sets of 3 vertices—say 3 houses $\{h_1, h_2, h_3\}$ and 3 utilities $\{u_1, u_2, u_3\}$. Every house connects to every utility, giving $V = 3 + 3 = 6$ vertices and $E = 3 \times 3 = 9$ edges.
  • Notice that $K_5$ has 10 edges, whereas $K_{3,3}$ has only 9 edges!
  • Why can't a non-planar graph have 8 or fewer edges?
    • If a graph had $V \le 4$, it is planar (subgraph of $K_4$).
    • If a graph has $V = 5$, it is obtained from $K_5$ (10 edges) by removing edges. Removing even one edge from $K_5$ (leaving 9 edges) immediately allows the trapped 5th chord to disappear, making $K_5 - e$ planar! So any graph on 5 vertices with $\le 9$ edges is planar.
    • For $V \ge 6$, any non-planar graph must contain a structure equivalent to $K_5$ (needs $\ge 10$ edges) or $K_{3,3}$ (needs $\ge 9$ edges).
  • Thus, no graph with $E \le 8$ edges can ever be non-planar, making $K_{3,3}$ ($E = 9$) the minimal-edge non-planar graph.
Topic 3

Regions, Euler's Theorem ($V - E + F = 2$), & Degree Bounds

3a. Concept of Regions (Faces) and Face-Degree Handshaking

When we draw a plane graph $G$ on a flat sheet of paper, the edges act like fences that divide the 2D plane into separate areas called regions or faces (denoted by $F$). Two points in the plane belong to the same face if you can draw a curve between them without crossing any edge or vertex of $G$.

  • Bounded vs. Unbounded (Exterior) Face: Every finite plane graph creates some number of enclosed interior regions (bounded faces) plus exactly one infinite region on the outside surrounding the entire drawing, called the unbounded or exterior face ($f_{\text{ext}}$). We always count this exterior face in $F$! (For example, a single triangle $C_3$ has $F = 2$ faces: 1 inside and 1 outside).
  • Degree (Length) of a Face $\deg(f)$: The degree of a face $f$ is the number of edge steps in the closed walk that traces around the boundary of $f$.
  • The Face Handshaking Lemma: Why does adding up the degrees of all faces always equal $2E$? Because in any plane graph, every edge $e$ either separates two distinct faces (so it gets counted once on the boundary of face A and once on the boundary of face B), OR $e$ is a "bridge/cut-edge" sticking out into a single face (so a walk tracing the boundary of that face travels down one side of $e$ and back along the other side, counting $e$ twice). Therefore:
$$ \sum_{f \in F} \deg(f) = 2E $$

3b. Euler's Theorem ($V - E + F = 2$): Explanation & Proof

Euler's Formula (1752): For any connected plane graph with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges, and $F$ faces, the following equation always holds:

$$ V - E + F = 2 $$

Step-by-Step Proof by Induction on the Number of Edges $E$:

Fix a connected plane graph with $V$ vertices. Since the graph is connected, the minimum possible number of edges it can have is $E = V - 1$ (when it is a tree).

  1. Base Case ($E = V - 1$, when $G$ is a Tree): If $G$ has $E = V - 1$ edges and is connected, $G$ is a tree. A tree contains no cycles, so it cannot enclose any interior regions! The only face is the single unbounded exterior face, meaning $F = 1$. Let's substitute $E = V - 1$ and $F = 1$ into Euler's formula: $$ V - E + F = V - (V - 1) + 1 = V - V + 1 + 1 = 2 $$ The base case holds!
  2. Inductive Hypothesis: Assume that $V - E + F = 2$ holds for any connected plane graph on $V$ vertices that has $E = k$ edges (where $k \ge V - 1$).
  3. Inductive Step ($E = k + 1$ edges): Let $G$ be a connected plane graph with $V$ vertices and $k + 1$ edges. Since $k + 1 > V - 1$, $G$ has more edges than a tree, which means $G$ must contain at least one cycle.
  4. Pick one edge $e$ that lies on a cycle in $G$. In the planar drawing, a cycle forms a closed loop, so the edge $e$ acts as a wall separating two distinct faces (one on each side of $e$).
  5. If we remove $e$ to form $G' = G - e$:
    • Removing an edge from a cycle does not disconnect the graph, so $G'$ is still connected.
    • The number of vertices doesn't change: $V' = V$.
    • The number of edges decreases by 1: $E' = (k + 1) - 1 = k$.
    • Because we removed the wall $e$ separating two faces, those two faces merge into one single face! So $G'$ has one fewer face: $F' = F - 1$.
  6. By our Inductive Hypothesis applied to $G'$ (which has $k$ edges): $$ V' - E' + F' = 2 $$ Substitute $V' = V$, $E' = E - 1$, and $F' = F - 1$: $$ V - (E - 1) + (F - 1) = 2 \implies V - E + 1 + F - 1 = 2 \implies V - E + F = 2 \quad \blacksquare $$

3c. Corollary 1: Proof of $E \le 3V - 6$

Statement: For any simple, connected planar graph with $V \ge 3$ vertices, $E \le 3V - 6$.

Step-by-Step Proof:

  1. Because the graph is simple (no self-loops of length 1 and no parallel double-edges of length 2) and has $V \ge 3$, every face (if bounded by a cycle, or even if it contains bridges) must be bounded by at least 3 edge steps: $$ \deg(f) \ge 3 \quad \text{for every face } f \in F $$
  2. Sum this inequality over all $F$ faces and apply the Face Handshaking Lemma ($\sum \deg(f) = 2E$): $$ 2E = \sum_{f \in F} \deg(f) \ge 3F \implies F \le \frac{2}{3}E $$
  3. Rearrange Euler's Formula ($V - E + F = 2$) to isolate $F$, then substitute $F \le \frac{2}{3}E$: $$ F = 2 - V + E \le \frac{2}{3}E $$
  4. Multiply the entire inequality by 3 to clear the fraction: $$ 6 - 3V + 3E \le 2E \implies E \le 3V - 6 \quad \blacksquare $$

Using this to prove $K_5$ is Non-Planar: For $K_5$, $V = 5$ and $E = 10$. If $K_5$ were planar, it would have to satisfy $E \le 3V - 6$. Plugging in the numbers gives $10 \le 3(5) - 6 = 9$, which says $10 \le 9$ (False!). Thus, $K_5$ cannot be planar.

3d. Corollary 2: Proof of $E \le 2V - 4$ (Triangle-Free)

Statement: For any simple, connected, triangle-free planar graph with $V \ge 3$ vertices, $E \le 2V - 4$.

Why do we need this? If we test $K_{3,3}$ ($V=6, E=9$) in Corollary 1 ($E \le 3V - 6$), we get $9 \le 3(6) - 6 = 12$, which is True! So Corollary 1 isn't sharp enough to catch $K_{3,3}$. However, $K_{3,3}$ is bipartite, and bipartite graphs have no odd cycles—specifically, no triangles ($C_3$)!

Step-by-Step Proof:

  1. Since the graph is simple and contains no triangles ($C_3$), the shortest possible cycle has length 4. Thus, every face needs at least 4 edge steps around its boundary: $$ \deg(f) \ge 4 \quad \text{for every face } f \in F $$
  2. Apply the Face Handshaking Lemma: $$ 2E = \sum_{f \in F} \deg(f) \ge 4F \implies F \le \frac{2}{4}E = \frac{1}{2}E $$
  3. Substitute $F \le \frac{1}{2}E$ into Euler's Formula ($F = 2 - V + E$): $$ 2 - V + E \le \frac{1}{2}E $$
  4. Multiply through by 2 and solve for $E$: $$ 4 - 2V + 2E \le E \implies E \le 2V - 4 \quad \blacksquare $$

Using this to prove $K_{3,3}$ is Non-Planar: For $K_{3,3}$, $V = 6, E = 9$, and it is triangle-free. Plugging into $E \le 2V - 4$ gives $9 \le 2(6) - 4 = 8$, which says $9 \le 8$ (False!). Thus, $K_{3,3}$ is non-planar.

3e. Proof that Every Simple Planar Graph Has a Vertex of Degree $\le 5$

Theorem: In every simple planar graph $G$, there is at least one vertex $v \in V(G)$ such that $\deg(v) \le 5$.

Proof by Contradiction:

  1. If $G$ has $V \le 2$ vertices, the maximum possible degree in a simple graph is $V - 1 \le 1 \le 5$, so the claim is trivially true. Now assume $V \ge 3$.
  2. Assume for the sake of contradiction that every vertex in $G$ has degree 6 or greater: $$ \deg(v) \ge 6 \quad \text{for all } v \in V(G) $$
  3. Recall the standard Vertex Handshaking Lemma, which states that adding up the degrees of all vertices counts every edge twice ($\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2E$). Summing $\deg(v) \ge 6$ over all $V$ vertices gives: $$ 2E = \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \ge \underbrace{6 + 6 + \dots + 6}_{V \text{ times}} = 6V $$ Dividing both sides by 2 yields: $$ E \ge 3V $$
  4. However, because $G$ is a simple planar graph with $V \ge 3$, Corollary 1 (from Topic 3c, which also holds if $G$ is disconnected by applying it to each component) requires that: $$ E \le 3V - 6 $$
  5. Combining both inequalities gives: $$ 3V \le E \le 3V - 6 \implies 0 \le -6 $$ This is a clear contradiction ($\Rightarrow\Leftarrow$)! Therefore, our assumption that all vertices have degree $\ge 6$ must be false. At least one vertex must have $\deg(v) \le 5$. $\blacksquare$
Topic 4

Kuratowski's Theorem, Series Reduction, & The Petersen Graph

4a. Why $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ are Regular, Non-Planar, and Critically Non-Planar

Property Complete Graph $K_5$ Complete Bipartite Graph $K_{3,3}$
Vertices & Edges $V = 5, \quad E = 10$ $V = 6, \quad E = 9$
Regularity (All vertices have the exact same degree) 4-Regular: Every vertex connects to the other 4 vertices, so $\deg(v) = 4$ for all $v$. 3-Regular (Cubic): Every vertex in set $U$ connects to all 3 vertices in set $W$, so $\deg(v) = 3$ for all $v$.
Non-Planarity Violates $E \le 3V - 6$ ($10 \not\le 9$). Triangle-free and violates $E \le 2V - 4$ ($9 \not\le 8$).
Critically Non-Planar (Minimal Obstruction) Removing any single edge $e$ (or vertex) leaves $K_5 - e$ ($E=9$), allowing the single crossing edge to be removed so the remaining graph is immediately planar! Removing any single edge $e$ (or vertex) leaves $K_{3,3} - e$ ($E=8$), which immediately untangles into a planar graph!

4d & 4b. Edge Subdivisions, Series Reduction, and Kuratowski's Theorem

Why aren't $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ the only two non-planar graphs in existence? Because if you take $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ and simply place extra dots along their edges, the crossing problem doesn't change at all! This leads to two inverse operations:

Edge Subdivision (Adding Degree-2 Vertices):

To subdivide an edge $e = uv$, we delete the edge $uv$, add a brand-new vertex $w$ in the middle, and connect $u - w - v$ with two edges. Notice that the new vertex $w$ has $\deg(w) = 2$. A subdivision of a graph $H$ is any graph obtained by performing zero or more edge subdivisions on $H$.

Series Reduction / Smoothing (Removing Degree-2 Vertices):

Series reduction is the exact reverse of subdivision! If a vertex $w$ has $\deg(w) = 2$ with neighbors $u$ and $v$ (where $u$ and $v$ are not already connected), we can "smooth out" $w$ by deleting $w$ and merging the two edges $(u,w)$ and $(w,v)$ into a single continuous edge $(u,v)$.

Adding or smoothing degree-2 vertices along an edge only stretches or shortens a curve on paper—it never changes whether a graph can be drawn without crossings. In 1930, Polish mathematician Kazimierz Kuratowski proved that $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ (and their subdivisions) are the only fundamental causes of non-planarity:

Kuratowski's Theorem (1930): A finite graph $G$ is planar if and only if $G$ does not contain a subgraph that is a subdivision of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$.

4c. Procedural Proof: Showing the Petersen Graph is Non-Planar via Kuratowski's Theorem

The Petersen Graph has $V = 10$ vertices and $E = 15$ edges, drawn as an outer pentagon ($0, 1, 2, 3, 4$) connected via 5 spokes to an inner 5-pointed star ($5, 6, 7, 8, 9$, where $0$ connects to $5$, $1$ to $6$, $2$ to $7$, $3$ to $8$, and $4$ to $9$). How do we systematically find a subdivision of $K_{3,3}$ inside it?

  1. Step 1 — Delete 1 Vertex and 1 Extra Edge to Form a Subgraph $H$:
    • First, delete vertex $3$ (one of the outer pentagon vertices) along with its three incident edges: $(2,3)$, $(3,4)$, and $(3,8)$.
    • Also delete the single outer edge $(0,1)$. What remains is a valid subgraph $H$ of the Petersen graph with $V = 9$ vertices and $E = 11$ edges.
  2. Step 2 — Identify the Two Bipartite Vertex Sets $U$ and $W$ (Each of Size 3):
    • Color 3 vertices Cyan as set $U = \{0, 1, 6\}$.
    • Color 3 vertices Rose as set $W = \{5, 7, 9\}$.
  3. Step 3 — Check the Remaining 3 Vertices ($2, 4,$ and $8$):
    • Look at the 3 leftover vertices $\{2, 4, 8\}$ in our subgraph $H$. Because we deleted vertex $3$ and its 3 edges, each of $\{2, 4, 8\}$ lost one of its original 3 edges and now has degree 2!
    • Specifically:
      • Vertex $4$ ($\deg = 2$) sits on the 2-edge path $0 - 4 - 9$ connecting $0 \in U$ to $9 \in W$.
      • Vertex $2$ ($\deg = 2$) sits on the 2-edge path $1 - 2 - 7$ connecting $1 \in U$ to $7 \in W$.
      • Vertex $8$ ($\deg = 2$) sits on the 2-edge path $6 - 8 - 5$ connecting $6 \in U$ to $5 \in W$.
  4. Step 4 — Apply Series Reduction to Smooth Out $\{2, 4, 8\}$:
    • Smooth out the three degree-2 vertices $4, 2,$ and $8$, replacing the paths $0-4-9$, $1-2-7$, and $6-8-5$ with direct edges $(0,9)$, $(1,7)$, and $(6,5)$.
    • Now check all connections between $U = \{0, 1, 6\}$ and $W = \{5, 7, 9\}$:
      • Vertex $0 \in U$ connects to $5$ (spoke), $7$ (via $5-7$? Wait: let's verify all 9 edges!).

Complete 9-Path Verification Table for the $K_{3,3}$ Subdivision in Petersen:

Even simpler: if you delete only vertex $3$ (and its 3 edges $(2,3), (3,4), (3,8)$) from the Petersen graph, the remaining 9 vertices consist of:

  • Set $U = \{0, 2, 6\}$ and Set $W = \{1, 5, 9\}$, with degree-2 subdivision vertices $\{4, 7, 8\}$!
  • From $0 \in U$: direct edge $(0,1)$ to $1$; direct spoke $(0,5)$ to $5$; subdivided path $0 - \mathbf{4} - 9$ to $9$. (Connected to all of $W$!)
  • From $2 \in U$: direct edge $(2,1)$ to $1$; subdivided path $2 - \mathbf{7} - 5$ to $5$; subdivided path $2 - \mathbf{7} - 9$? No—use $U = \{1, 4, 8\}$ and $W = \{0, 2, 6\}$ or contract the 5 spokes!

Let's write down the 100% exact edge-by-edge $K_{3,3}$ subdivision inside the Petersen graph so you can verify every single path on paper:

Exact Bipartition Sets in Petersen ($V=10, E=15$):

Delete outer vertex $3$ and its 3 incident edges $(2,3), (3,4), (3,8)$, plus delete the single inner star edge $(5,8)$? No—in a $K_{3,3}$ subdivision, the 6 main "branch" vertices have degree 3, and the remaining vertices have degree 2! Since $K_{3,3}$ has 9 edges, if we subdivide 3 of its edges (adding 3 degree-2 vertices), the subdivided $K_{3,3}$ has $6 + 3 = 9$ vertices and $9 + 3 = 12$ edges. When we delete vertex $3$ and its 3 incident edges from the Petersen graph ($10 - 1 = 9$ vertices, $15 - 3 = 12$ edges), every single remaining edge is used—zero extra edges need to be deleted!

Here are the exact 6 degree-3 vertices ($U$ and $W$) and the 3 degree-2 vertices after deleting vertex $3$:

  • When vertex $3$ is deleted, its 3 neighbors $2, 4,$ and $8$ drop from degree 3 to degree 2! So the 3 degree-2 subdivision vertices are must be $\{2, 4, 8\}$.
  • The remaining 6 vertices Still have degree 3! They form our two bipartition sets:
    Set $U = \{0, 6, 7\}$ and Set $W = \{1, 5, 9\}$.
  • Let's check all 9 paths between $U = \{0, 6, 7\}$ and $W = \{1, 5, 9\}$ (using all 12 remaining edges):
    1. From $0 \in U$: connects to $1 \in W$ via edge $(0,1)$; connects to $5 \in W$ via spoke $(0,5)$; connects to $9 \in W$ via path $0 - \mathbf{4} - 9$ (smoothing degree-2 vertex $4$).
    2. From $6 \in U$: connects to $1 \in W$ via spoke $(6,1)$; connects to $5 \in W$ via path $6 - \mathbf{8} - 5$ (smoothing degree-2 vertex $8$); connects to $9 \in W$ via inner star edge $(6,9)$.
    3. From $7 \in U$: connects to $1 \in W$ via path $7 - \mathbf{2} - 1$ (smoothing degree-2 vertex $2$); connects to $5 \in W$ via inner star edge $(7,5)$; connects to $9 \in W$ via inner star edge $(7,9)$.

Every vertex in $U = \{0, 6, 7\}$ connects to every vertex in $W = \{1, 5, 9\}$ via completely edge-disjoint paths! Smoothing $\{2, 4, 8\}$ via series reduction produces $K_{3,3}$ directly. By Kuratowski's Theorem, the Petersen graph is non-planar. $\blacksquare$

Topic 5

Dual Graphs ($G^*$) of Planar Graphs: Full Explanation

Every plane graph $G$ (a planar graph drawn with a specific crossing-free embedding) has a companion graph called its geometric dual graph, denoted $G^*$. Intuitively, if you think of the faces of $G$ as countries on a map and the edges of $G$ as borders between countries, the dual graph $G^*$ places a capital city inside each country and draws a road across every border connecting neighboring capitals!

Step-by-Step Construction of the Dual Graph $G^*$

  1. Step 1 (Faces $\to$ Dual Vertices): Inside every face $f$ of the plane graph $G$—including the unbounded exterior face $f_{\text{ext}}$—place exactly one dual vertex $v_f^*$.
  2. Step 2 (Primal Edges $\to$ Dual Edges): For every edge $e$ in the original graph $G$, look at the faces on either side of $e$:
    • If $e$ separates two distinct faces $f_1$ and $f_2$, draw a dual edge $e^*$ connecting the dual vertex $v_{f_1}^*$ to $v_{f_2}^*$ so that $e^*$ crosses only the edge $e$ (and no other edges).
    • Special Case (Bridge / Cut-edge): What if an edge $e$ in $G$ is a bridge that has the same face $f_1$ on both sides of it? Then the dual edge $e^*$ crosses $e$ and connects $v_{f_1}^*$ back to itself, forming a self-loop in $G^*$!
    • Special Case (Two faces sharing multiple edges): If two faces $f_1$ and $f_2$ share $k$ different boundary edges between them, then $v_{f_1}^*$ and $v_{f_2}^*$ will be connected by $k$ parallel (multiple) edges in $G^*$. Thus, $G^*$ is often a pseudograph / multigraph even when $G$ is a simple graph!

Fundamental Properties of the Dual Graph $G^*$

$$ V^* = F, \qquad E^* = E, \qquad F^* = V, \qquad \deg_{G^*}(v_f^*) = \deg_G(f) $$
  • Planarity of $G^*$: The dual graph $G^*$ can always be drawn without its dual edges crossing each other, so $G^*$ is always a planar (multi)graph.
  • Duality is Symmetric for Connected Graphs ($G^{**} \cong G$): If $G$ is a connected plane graph, taking the dual of the dual ($G^{**}$) gives you back a graph isomorphic to the original graph $G$! Why? Because inside each face of $G^*$, there sits exactly one original vertex of $G$.
  • Self-Dual Graphs ($G \cong G^*$): A plane graph is called self-dual if its dual graph $G^*$ is isomorphic to $G$ itself (which immediately requires $V = F$, so by Euler's formula $2V - E = 2$).
    • Classic Example: Any Wheel Graph $W_n$ (including the tetrahedron $K_4 = W_3$) is self-dual! For instance, in $K_4$, there are $V = 4$ vertices, $E = 6$ edges, and $F = 4$ triangular faces (3 interior triangles + 1 exterior triangle). Placing a dual vertex in each of the 4 faces and connecting them across the 6 edges produces another $K_4$!
  • Important Subtlety — Duals Depend on the Embedding: If a planar graph $G$ can be drawn in two topologically different ways in the plane (which can happen when $G$ is not 3-connected), those two different plane drawings of the same graph $G$ can actually produce non-isomorphic dual graphs!
Special Topic

Proving Every Planar Graph is 5-Colorable

A proper $k$-coloring of a graph $G$ assigns one of $k$ colors to each vertex so that no two adjacent vertices (vertices connected by an edge) have the same color. While the famous 4-Color Theorem requires extensive computer case-checking to prove, the 5-Color Theorem (proved by Percy John Heawood in 1890) has one of the most elegant human proofs in all of mathematics, combining Topic 3e with a clever trick called Kempe Chains.

Complete Step-by-Step Proof by Induction on $V$

  1. Base Case ($V \le 5$): If a graph has 5 or fewer vertices, we can simply give every vertex its own unique color from $\{c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5\}$. Thus, it is trivially 5-colorable.
  2. Inductive Hypothesis: Assume that every simple planar graph with $k$ vertices ($k \ge 5$) can be properly colored using at most 5 colors.
  3. Inductive Step ($V = k + 1$): Let $G$ be a simple planar graph with $k + 1$ vertices. By Topic 3e, we proved that $G$ is guaranteed to contain at least one vertex $v$ with $\deg(v) \le 5$.
  4. Temporarily remove $v$ (and its incident edges) to form $G' = G - v$. Since $G'$ is a planar graph with $k$ vertices, by our Inductive Hypothesis, $G'$ has a valid 5-coloring using colors $\{c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5\}$. Now we put $v$ back into the graph and find a valid color for $v$:

Case 1 (The Easy Case): $\deg(v) \le 4$, OR $\deg(v) = 5$ with a Repeated Color

If $v$ has at most 4 neighbors, or if $v$ has 5 neighbors but two of them share the same color, then the neighbors of $v$ use at most 4 distinct colors out of the 5 available colors $\{c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5\}$. That leaves at least one unused color! We simply assign that unused color to $v$, and we are done.

Case 2 (The Hard Case): $\deg(v) = 5$ and All 5 Neighbors Have Different Colors

Suppose $v$ has exactly 5 neighbors, and in our planar drawing of $G$, those 5 neighbors appear around $v$ in clockwise circular order as $v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4, v_5$, colored with all 5 distinct colors:

$v_1: c_1$  |  $v_2: c_2$  |  $v_3: c_3$  |  $v_4: c_4$  |  $v_5: c_5$

Since all 5 colors are taken by $v$'s neighbors, we must slightly modify the coloring of $G - v$ to free up a color for $v$. To do this, look at two non-adjacent neighbors around the circle: $v_1$ (color $c_1$) and $v_3$ (color $c_3$).

Define the Kempe Subgraph $G_{1,3}$ to be the subgraph of $G - v$ consisting only of all vertices in the graph that are currently colored $c_1$ or $c_3$ (and the edges between them). There are only two possibilities:

  • Subcase 2A ($v_1$ and $v_3$ are in DIFFERENT connected components of $G_{1,3}$):
    There is no alternating $c_1 - c_3$ path connecting $v_1$ to $v_3$. Take only the connected component of $G_{1,3}$ that contains $v_1$, and swap colors $c_1 \leftrightarrow c_3$ on every vertex inside that component!
    Why is this still a valid coloring? Any edge inside that component just swaps its two endpoints from $(c_1, c_3)$ to $(c_3, c_1)$, and any vertex outside the component either has a completely different color ($c_2, c_4, c_5$) or isn't connected to this component at all.
    What did this achieve? Vertex $v_1$ changed from $c_1$ to $c_3$, while $v_3$ (being in a separate component) stayed $c_3$! Now both $v_1$ and $v_3$ have color $c_3$, meaning color $c_1$ is completely free for $v$!
  • Subcase 2B ($v_1$ and $v_3$ are in the SAME connected component of $G_{1,3}$):
    If Subcase 2A doesn't work, it means there is an alternating $c_1 - c_3$ path $P_{1,3}$ in $G - v$ that starts at $v_1$ and ends at $v_3$.
    Now look at the closed loop formed by $v \to v_1 \leadsto P_{1,3} \leadsto v_3 \to v$. Because $G$ is drawn in the plane without edge crossings, this closed loop forms a wall in the plane!
    Because the neighbors $v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4, v_5$ are arranged in clockwise order around $v$, this loop traps $v_2$ on the inside and leaves $v_4$ on the outside (or vice versa).
    Now try the exact same Kempe chain trick on $v_2$ (color $c_2$) and $v_4$ (color $c_4$) using the subgraph $G_{2,4}$ of all vertices colored $c_2$ or $c_4$. Could there be a path $P_{2,4}$ of $c_2 - c_4$ vertices connecting $v_2$ to $v_4$?
    NO! To get from the inside ($v_2$) to the outside ($v_4$) without edges crossing, the path $P_{2,4}$ would have to pass through a vertex of the boundary loop $P_{1,3}$. But every vertex on $P_{1,3}$ is colored $c_1$ or $c_3$, whereas every vertex on $P_{2,4}$ is colored $c_2$ or $c_4$! They have no colors in common ($\{c_1, c_3\} \cap \{c_2, c_4\} = \emptyset$), so they cannot share a vertex!
    Therefore, $v_2$ and $v_4$ are 100% guaranteed to be in different connected components of $G_{2,4}$! We simply swap colors $c_2 \leftrightarrow c_4$ in $v_2$'s component of $G_{2,4}$, which changes $v_2$ to color $c_4$, leaves $v_4$ as $c_4$, and frees up color $c_2$ for our center vertex $v$! $\blacksquare$
