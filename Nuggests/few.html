Result 1 Handshaking Lemma

Theorem (Handshaking Lemma)

For every finite graph $G = (V, E)$,

$$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \;=\; 2\,|E|.$$

Proof (Double Counting Incidences)

Consider the set of ordered pairs representing vertex-edge incidences:

$$S = \{(v, e) \in V \times E : v \text{ is an endpoint of } e\}.$$

We count $|S|$ in two ways:

  1. By Vertices: A vertex $v$ appears in exactly $\deg(v)$ pairs, hence $|S| = \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)$.
  2. By Edges: An edge $e = \{x, y\}$ gives exactly two pairs, $(x, e)$ and $(y, e)$, hence $|S| = 2\,|E|$.

Equating both counts gives $\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2\,|E|$.

$\square$
$\deg A = 3, \deg B = 2, \deg C = 3, \deg D = 2$; sum $= 10 = 2 \cdot 5$.

Result 2 Parity of Odd-Degree Vertices

Corollary

The number of vertices of odd degree in any finite graph is even.

Proof

Split the handshaking sum into even-degree and odd-degree vertices:

$$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_{\deg\text{ even}} \deg(v)}_{\text{even}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{\deg\text{ odd}} \deg(v)}_{\equiv\, |V_{\text{odd}}| \pmod 2}.$$

Reducing modulo $2$:

$$0 \;\equiv\; \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \;\equiv\; |V_{\text{odd}}| \pmod 2.$$

Hence $|V_{\text{odd}}|$ is even.

$\square$
Odd-degree vertices (red): $A$ and $C$ ($\vert{}V_{\text{odd}}\vert{} = 2$).

Result 3 Maximum Edges in a Bipartite Graph

Theorem (Bipartite Edge Maximum)

If $G$ is a simple bipartite graph on $n$ vertices, then

$$|E(G)| \;\le\; \left\lfloor \frac{n^{2}}{4} \right\rfloor.$$

Proof

Setup: Let $V = A \cup B$ with $|A| = t$ and $|B| = n - t$. Since $G$ is bipartite, no edges lie inside $A$ or inside $B$, hence $|E(G)| \le |A| \cdot |B| = t(n - t)$.

Maximise Quadratic: Define $f(t) = t(n - t) = \frac{n^2}{4} - \left(t - \frac{n}{2}\right)^2$. This is a downward parabola with vertex at $t = n/2$. Over integers $0 \le t \le n$:

$$\max_{0 \le t \le n} f(t) \;=\; f\left(\left\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \right\rfloor\right) \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{n^2}{4} \right\rfloor.$$

Attainment: The complete bipartite graph $K_{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor, \lceil n/2 \rceil}$ achieves equality.

$\square$
$K_{3,3}$: $n = 6$, edges $= 9 = \lfloor 36/4 \rfloor$.

Result 4 Bipartite Characterisation ($\Rightarrow$)

Theorem (Bipartite Characterisation)

A graph $G$ is bipartite if and only if $G$ contains no cycle of odd length.

Proof ($\Rightarrow$: Bipartite implies no odd cycle)

Let $G$ be bipartite with parts $A, B$. Take any cycle $v_1 v_2 \dots v_k v_1$. Each edge joins $A$ to $B$, so vertex colors alternate along the cycle:

$$v_1 \in A,\quad v_2 \in B,\quad v_3 \in A,\quad v_4 \in B, \quad \dots$$

Odd-indexed vertices lie in $A$, even-indexed vertices lie in $B$. Since $v_k$ is adjacent to $v_1 \in A$, $v_k$ must belong to $B$, which requires $k$ to be even. Hence, every cycle in $G$ has even length.

$\square$
$C_5$ (odd, non-bipartite) vs $C_6$ (even, bipartite).

Result 5 Bipartite Characterisation ($\Leftarrow$)

Proof ($\Leftarrow$: No odd cycle implies Bipartite) — Detailed Breakdown

Assume every cycle of $G$ has even length. It suffices to treat one connected component (then union over components).

Fix a vertex $v$ in that component and define two sets based on shortest-path distance $d(v, x)$:

$$V_1 = \{x \in V : d(v, x) \text{ is even}\}, \qquad V_2 = \{x \in V : d(v, x) \text{ is odd}\}.$$

Clearly $V_1 \cup V_2 = V$ and $V_1 \cap V_2 = \emptyset$.

Claim: No two vertices of $V_1$ are adjacent.

Use of Hypothesis: Suppose $x, y \in V_1$ with $xy \in E$. Let $P_x$ and $P_y$ be shortest $v$-$x$ and $v$-$y$ paths. Both paths have even length (by definition of $V_1$).

Then the closed walk $W = P_x + xy + \text{reverse}(P_y)$ has length:

$$\text{length}(W) = \text{even} + 1 + \text{even} = \text{odd}.$$

A closed odd walk always contains an odd cycle (splitting the walk at any repeated vertex leaves at least one piece of odd length). But our hypothesis states that $G$ contains no odd cycles — a contradiction!

Hence, no two vertices of $V_1$ are adjacent. By identical reasoning, no two vertices of $V_2$ are adjacent. Therefore, every edge joins $V_1$ to $V_2$, proving $(V_1, V_2)$ is a valid bipartition.

$\square$
Edge inside $V_1$ creates an odd closed walk, forcing an odd cycle.

Result 6 Eulerian Circuit Condition

Theorem (Eulerian Criterion)

A connected graph has a closed trail using every edge exactly once (an Eulerian circuit) iff every vertex has even degree.

Proof (Necessity & Sufficiency)

$(\Rightarrow)$ Necessity: Let $C$ be an Eulerian circuit. Each pass through $v \neq r$ enters by one edge and leaves by another. The initial departure and final return at base vertex $r$ pair up as well. Thus $\deg(v) = 2 \cdot (\text{visits})$, which is even.

$(\Leftarrow)$ Sufficiency (Hierholzer's Algorithm):

  1. Greedy Trail: Start at any vertex and follow unused edges. Since every degree is even, the walk can only terminate by returning to the start, giving closed trail $C$.
  2. Splice: If unused edges remain in $H = G \setminus E(C)$, select $u \in C$ touching $H$, trace closed trail $C'$ in $H$, and splice $C'$ into $C$ at $u$.
  3. Iterate: Repeat until all edges are traversed.
$\square$
Figure-eight: $\deg(v) = 4$, all other degrees $= 2$.

Hamiltonian Graphs Ore's Theorem & Dirac's Corollary

Theorem (Ore, 1960)

Let $G = (V, E)$ be a simple graph on $n \ge 3$ vertices. If

$$\deg(u) + \deg(v) \;\ge\; n$$

for every pair of non-adjacent vertices $u, v \in V$, then $G$ contains a Hamiltonian cycle.

Elaborated Proof of Ore's Theorem
  1. 1. Extremal Setup: Suppose $G$ satisfies Ore's condition but is non-Hamiltonian. Choose $G$ to be edge-maximal with respect to this property.
  2. 2. Add Missing Edge & Extract Path: For non-adjacent $u, v \in V$, adding $uv$ creates a Hamiltonian cycle in $G + uv$. Deleting $uv$ leaves a Hamiltonian path $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ in $G$ with $v_1 = u, v_n = v$ ($v_1 \nsim v_n$).
  3. 3. Crossing Edge Exclusion: Set $S = \{i : v_1 \sim v_i\}$ with $|S| = \deg(v_1)$. If $v_1 \sim v_i$, then $v_n \nsim v_{i-1}$; otherwise, the sequence $v_1 \to v_i \to v_{i+1} \dots \to v_n \to v_{i-1} \to \dots \to v_1$ forms a Hamiltonian cycle in $G$, a contradiction!
  4. 4. Counting Contradiction: For each neighbor $v_i$ of $v_1$, $v_{i-1}$ is a non-neighbor of $v_n$. Including $v_1$ itself, $v_n$ has at least $\deg(v_1) + 1$ non-neighbors. Thus $\deg(v_n) \le (n - 1) - (\deg(v_1) + 1) \implies \deg(v_1) + \deg(v_n) \le n - 2$, contradicting $\deg(v_1) + \deg(v_n) \ge n$.
$\square$
Hamiltonian path with non-adjacent $v_1, v_n$. Crossing edges $v_1 v_i$ and $v_{i-1} v_n$ (red) would close a Hamiltonian cycle.
Corollary (Dirac, 1952)

Let $G = (V, E)$ be a simple graph on $n \ge 3$ vertices. If $\deg(v) \ge n/2$ for every vertex $v \in V$, then $G$ contains a Hamiltonian cycle.

Proof (As a Direct Corollary to Ore's Theorem)
  1. 1. Apply Dirac's Hypothesis: Let $u, v \in V$ be any pair of non-adjacent vertices in $G$. By Dirac's condition:

    $$\deg(u) \;\ge\; \frac{n}{2} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \deg(v) \;\ge\; \frac{n}{2}.$$

  2. 2. Sum the Degrees: Adding both bounds yields:

    $$\deg(u) + \deg(v) \;\ge\; \frac{n}{2} + \frac{n}{2} \;=\; n.$$

  3. 3. Conclusion via Ore's Theorem: Since $\deg(u) + \deg(v) \ge n$ holds for every non-adjacent pair, Ore's condition is satisfied. By Ore's Theorem, $G$ contains a Hamiltonian cycle.
$\square$
Remark on Sharpness

The bound $n/2$ is sharp: For even $n$, the disjoint union $K_{n/2} \cup K_{n/2}$ has minimum degree $\frac{n}{2} - 1$ and is disconnected / non-Hamiltonian.

3-regular graph ($n = 6, \deg(v) \ge 3 = 6/2$), guaranteeing a Hamiltonian cycle via Dirac & Ore conditions.

Result 8 Euler's Planar Formula ($v - e + f = 2$)

Theorem (Euler's Planar Formula)

For a connected plane graph with $v$ vertices, $e$ edges, and $f$ faces,

$$v - e + f \;=\; 2.$$

Proof (Induction on Edges)

Base Case ($G$ is a tree): $e = v - 1$ and $f = 1$ (only the outer unbounded face exists). Thus $v - e + f = v - (v - 1) + 1 = 2$.

Inductive Step: Suppose $G$ contains a cycle and pick edge $e_0$ on it.

  • Removing $e_0$ leaves $G - e_0$ connected (detour exists along cycle).
  • The two faces bordering $e_0$ merge into one, so $f$ decreases by 1 and $e$ decreases by 1, while $v$ is unchanged.

The quantity $v - e + f$ is invariant under removing cycle edges. By induction, $v - e + f = 2$.

$\square$
$v = 4, e = 6, f = 4 \implies 4 - 6 + 4 = 2$.

Result 9 Planar Edge Bound ($e \le 3n - 6$)

Theorem (Planar Edge Bound)

For a simple connected planar graph on $n \ge 3$ vertices with $e$ edges,

$$e \;\le\; 3n - 6.$$

Proof

Face Lengths $\ge 3$: Since $G$ is simple and $n \ge 3$, every face boundary has length $\ge 3$.

Double Counting: Summing face lengths counts each edge twice: $\sum_F \ell(F) = 2e \ge 3f \implies f \le \frac{2e}{3}$.

Combine with Euler: Substitute $f = e - n + 2$ into the inequality:

$$e - n + 2 \;\le\; \frac{2e}{3} \;\implies\; 3e - 3n + 6 \;\le\; 2e \;\implies\; e \;\le\; 3n - 6.$$

$\square$
$K_5$: $n = 5, e = 10 > 9 = 3n - 6$ (Non-planar).

Result 10 Girth and Planar Bound

Theorem (Girth Bound)

Let $G$ be simple, connected, planar with $n$ vertices, $e$ edges, and girth $r \ge 3$. Then

$$e \;\le\; \frac{r(n - 2)}{r - 2}.$$

Proof

Since girth is $r$, every face has boundary length $\ge r$. Thus $2e = \sum_F \ell(F) \ge rf \implies f \le \frac{2e}{r}$.

Substituting $f = e - n + 2$ from Euler's formula:

$$e - n + 2 \;\le\; \frac{2e}{r} \;\implies\; re - rn + 2r \;\le\; 2e \;\implies\; e(r - 2) \;\le\; r(n - 2).$$

Dividing by $(r - 2)$ gives $e \le \frac{r(n - 2)}{r - 2}$.

$\square$

Result 11 The Petersen Graph is Not Hamiltonian

Theorem (Petersen, 1898)

The Petersen graph $P$ has no Hamiltonian cycle: there is no cycle in $P$ that visits all $10$ vertices exactly once.

Proof (Structure of a Hypothetical Hamiltonian Cycle)

Setup. Write $P$ as two $5$-cycles joined by a perfect matching of five "spokes":

$$A = a_0a_1a_2a_3a_4a_0, \qquad B = b_0b_2b_4b_1b_3b_0, \qquad M = \{a_ib_i : i = 0,\dots,4\}.$$

The index rules for the two cycles are different, and this is the crux:

$$a_ia_j \in E(A) \iff i - j \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 5, \qquad b_ib_j \in E(B) \iff i - j \equiv \pm 2 \pmod 5.$$

Suppose, for contradiction, that $C$ is a Hamiltonian cycle of $P$. The matching $M$ is the only connection between $A$ and $B$, so every time $C$ leaves $A$ it must return: $C$ uses an even number of spokes. Since $C$ cannot use $0$ spokes (it would be trapped in $A \cup B$, which is disconnected), $C$ uses $2$ or $4$ spokes.

  1. Case 1: $C$ uses exactly $2$ spokes, say $a_ib_i$ and $a_jb_j$. Then $C \cap A$ is a single path on all $5$ vertices of $A$, i.e. a Hamiltonian path of the $5$-cycle $A$ with endpoints $a_i, a_j$. In a $5$-cycle the endpoints of a Hamiltonian path are always adjacent, so $i - j \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 5$. Likewise $C \cap B$ is a Hamiltonian path of $B$ with the same index endpoints $b_i, b_j$, forcing $i - j \equiv \pm 2 \pmod 5$. Contradiction.
  2. Case 2: $C$ uses exactly $4$ spokes. Then exactly one spoke $a_mb_m$ is unused. Since $a_m$ has degree $2$ in $C$ and neither of its $C$-edges is in $M$, both lie in $A$: the vertex $a_m$ is internal to a path of $C \cap A$. Hence that path must run through both $A$-neighbours of $a_m$, i.e. it is $a_{m-1}a_ma_{m+1}$, and the remaining two vertices $a_{m+2}, a_{m+3}$ form the other $A$-path. By the same reasoning applied to $B$ (whose neighbours of $b_m$ are $b_{m\pm 2}$), we get $$C \cap B = \bigl\{\, b_{m+2}b_mb_{m-2},\; b_{m+1}b_{m-1} \,\bigr\}.$$ But now the endpoints $a_{m+1}, a_{m-1}$ of the first $A$-path are matched to $b_{m+1}, b_{m-1}$ — exactly the endpoints of the second $B$-path. So $C$ contains the closed $5$-cycle $$a_{m-1}\,a_m\,a_{m+1}\,b_{m+1}\,b_{m-1}\,a_{m-1},$$ and the remaining six edges of $C$ close up into the second $5$-cycle $a_{m+2}a_{m+3}b_{m+3}b_mb_{m+2}a_{m+2}$. Thus $C$ is a disjoint union of two $5$-cycles, not a $10$-cycle. Contradiction.

Both possibilities are impossible, so $P$ admits no Hamiltonian cycle.

$\square$
Petersen graph: outer $5$-cycle $A$ (blue), inner $5$-cycle $B$ (grey), and the perfect matching $M$ of five spokes (orange). Any Hamiltonian cycle would need $2$ or $4$ spokes — both lead to a contradiction.
Remarks

The Petersen graph is the smallest cubic non-Hamiltonian graph: $10$ vertices, $15$ edges, $3$-regular, girth $5$, non-planar, vertex-transitive and edge-transitive.

It is hypohamiltonian — deleting any single vertex leaves a graph that does have a Hamiltonian cycle — and it does possess a $2$-factor, namely the two disjoint $5$-cycles $A \cup B$.

Removing the $5$ spokes from a Hamiltonian-cycle attempt is precisely the trap exploited above: a perfect matching whose complement is two $5$-cycles rather than one $10$-cycle.

Result 11 The Petersen Graph is Not Planar

Theorem

The Petersen graph $P$ — $10$ vertices, $15$ edges, $3$-regular, girth $5$ — is not planar.

Proof 1 — Girth Bound (Edge Counting)

Step 1: the girth of $P$ is $5$. $P$ is vertex-transitive and the neighbourhood of any vertex is independent — e.g. $N(v_0) = \{v_1, v_4, u_0\}$ contains no edge — so $P$ is triangle-free. It also has no $4$-cycle: in $P$, any two non-adjacent vertices have exactly one common neighbour, whereas two opposite corners of a $4$-cycle would share two common neighbours. Finally, the outer pentagon $v_0v_1v_2v_3v_4v_0$ is a cycle of length $5$, so

$$r \;=\; \operatorname{girth}(P) \;=\; 5.$$

Step 2: the girth bound. Suppose, for contradiction, that $P$ were planar. Since $P$ is simple, connected and $n = 10 \ge 3$, Euler's formula $n - e + f = 2$ together with $2e = \sum_F \ell(F) \ge rf$ gives

$$f \;\le\; \frac{2e}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e - n + 2 \;\le\; \frac{2e}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e \;\le\; \frac{r(n-2)}{r-2}.$$

Step 3: contradiction. Substituting $r = 5$ and $n = 10$:

$$e \;\le\; \frac{5\,(10-2)}{5-2} \;=\; \frac{40}{3} \;=\; 13.\overline{3}.$$

But $P$ has $e = 15$ edges, and $15 > 40/3$. This contradiction shows that $P$ is not planar.

$\square$
The outer pentagon (red) is a cycle of length $5$; $P$ has no $3$- or $4$-cycle, so $\operatorname{girth}(P)=5$. Then $e \le \frac{5(10-2)}{3} = 13.\overline{3} < 15 = e(P)$.
Proof 2 — Kuratowski's Theorem (Topological $K_{3,3}$)

Kuratowski's theorem. A finite graph is planar if and only if it contains no subdivision of $K_5$ and no subdivision of $K_{3,3}$. It therefore suffices to exhibit a subdivision of $K_{3,3}$ inside $P$.

Presentation of $P$. Write

$$V(P) = \{v_0,\dots,v_4\} \cup \{u_0,\dots,u_4\},$$

with the two $5$-cycles $v_0v_1v_2v_3v_4v_0$ and $u_0u_2u_4u_1u_3u_0$, joined by the five spokes $v_ju_j$, $j = 0,\dots,4$.

Branch vertices. Take the two independent triples

$$A = \{v_1,\,u_3,\,u_4\}, \qquad B = \{v_4,\,u_2,\,u_1\}.$$

The remaining four vertices $v_0,\,v_2,\,v_3,\,u_0$ will serve as the internal vertices of the subdivision.

The nine paths. Each vertex of $A$ is joined to each vertex of $B$ by one of the following paths:

$v_4$$u_2$$u_1$
$v_1$$v_1 v_0 v_4$$v_1 v_2 u_2$$v_1 u_1$
$u_3$$u_3 v_3 v_4$$u_3 u_0 u_2$$u_3 u_1$
$u_4$$u_4 v_4$$u_4 u_2$$u_4 u_1$

Every entry of the table is indeed a path in $P$: the length-$1$ entries are edges of $P$, and the four length-$2$ entries pass through $v_0$, $v_2$, $v_3$, $u_0$ respectively — each internal vertex occurring in exactly one path. Hence the nine paths are pairwise internally disjoint, so they form a subdivision of $K_{3,3}$ with branch sets $A$ and $B$.

Conclusion. $P$ contains a subdivision of $K_{3,3}$; by Kuratowski's theorem, $P$ is not planar. (The two edges $v_0u_0$ and $v_2v_3$ of $P$ are simply not used by the subdivision.)

$\square$
A subdivision of $K_{3,3}$ inside $P$: branch vertices $A = \{v_1,u_3,u_4\}$ (red) and $B = \{v_4,u_2,u_1\}$ (green); the grey vertices $v_0,v_2,v_3,u_0$ are internal, each lying on exactly one of the nine disjoint $A$–$B$ paths. The dashed edges $v_0u_0$ and $v_2v_3$ are unused.
Remarks

The two arguments are logically independent: Proof 1 is purely analytic (Euler's formula plus the girth constraint), while Proof 2 is structural (Kuratowski's forbidden-subdivision criterion).

The same girth argument disposes of $K_{3,3}$ itself: its girth is $4$, so the bound gives $e \le \frac{4(6-2)}{4-2} = 8$, but $e(K_{3,3}) = 9$.

$P$ is the unique $(3,5)$-cage — the smallest $3$-regular graph of girth $5$ — and the smallest cubic graph that is not $3$-edge-colourable (the smallest snark).

Counting Walks in $K_4$

Setup

Let $A_n$ be the number of walks of length $n$ between two fixed distinct vertices $u$ and $v$ of $K_4$, and let $B_n$ be the number of closed walks of length $n$ starting and ending at $u$.

Because $K_4$ is vertex-transitive, $A_n$ depends only on $n$, not on the particular pair $(u,v)$.

Recurrences

A walk of length $n+1$ from $u$ to $v$ must arrive at $v$ from a neighbour of $v$ — that is, from $u$ or from one of the two remaining vertices:

$$A_{n+1} = B_n + 2A_n.$$

Likewise, a closed walk of length $n+1$ at $u$ must arrive from one of the three neighbours of $u$:

$$B_{n+1} = 3A_n.$$

Eliminating $B_n$ using $B_n = 3A_{n-1}$ (valid for $n \ge 1$) gives

$$A_{n+1} = 3A_{n-1} + 2A_n,$$

i.e. the linear recurrence $A_{n+1} - 2A_n - 3A_{n-1} = 0$.

Closed Form via the Characteristic Equation

The characteristic equation is

$$r^2 - 2r - 3 = 0,$$

with roots $r = 3$ and $r = -1$. Therefore

$$A_n = \alpha\,3^n + \beta\,(-1)^n$$

for constants $\alpha, \beta$.

Initial conditions: $A_0 = 0$ (no walk of length $0$ between distinct vertices) and $A_1 = 1$ (the single edge $uv$). Substituting:

$$\alpha + \beta = 0, \qquad 3\alpha - \beta = 1.$$

Solving gives $\alpha = \tfrac14$ and $\beta = -\tfrac14$. Hence

$$A_n = \frac{3^n - (-1)^n}{4}.$$

For $n = 2,3,4,5$ this yields $2, 7, 20, 61$, respectively.

Alternative Approach — Adjacency Matrix

Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of $K_4$. Then the $(i,j)$-entry of $A^n$ counts walks of length $n$ from vertex $i$ to vertex $j$, so $A_n$ is any off-diagonal entry of $A^n$ and $B_n$ is any diagonal entry.

$$A=\begin{pmatrix} 0&1&1&1\\ 1&0&1&1\\ 1&1&0&1\\ 1&1&1&0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^2=\begin{pmatrix} 3&2&2&2\\ 2&3&2&2\\ 2&2&3&2\\ 2&2&2&3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^3=\begin{pmatrix} 6&7&7&7\\ 7&6&7&7\\ 7&7&6&7\\ 7&7&7&6 \end{pmatrix},$$ $$A^4=\begin{pmatrix} 21&20&20&20\\ 20&21&20&20\\ 20&20&21&20\\ 20&20&20&21 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^5=\begin{pmatrix} 60&61&61&61\\ 61&60&61&61\\ 61&61&60&61\\ 61&61&61&60 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Reading off the off-diagonal entries of $A^n$ reproduces $A_n = 0, 1, 2, 7, 20, 61$ for $n = 0,1,\dots,5$, while the diagonal entries give $B_n = 1, 0, 3, 6, 21, 60$ — in agreement with the recurrence solution.

Result 11 Moore Graphs are Regular

Theorem (Moore Graph Regularity)

Let $X$ be a graph with diameter $d$ and girth $2d+1$ (such a graph is called a Moore graph). Then $X$ is regular — every vertex of $X$ has the same degree.

Proof
  1. 1. Tree-like neighbourhoods. Because the girth is $2d+1$, the graph $X$ contains no cycles of length $2d$ or less. Consequently, for any vertex $x \in X$, the neighbourhood structure up to distance $d$ forms a tree rooted at $x$. Since the diameter of $X$ is $d$, this tree spans every vertex of the graph exactly once.
  2. 2. Splitting along an edge. Let $u$ and $v$ be any two adjacent vertices in $X$ ($u \sim v$). The removal of the edge $uv$ partitions the graph into two disjoint sets of vertices:
    • $A$: the set of vertices closer to $u$ than to $v$;
    • $B$: the set of vertices closer to $v$ than to $u$.
    Since there are no cycles of length $\le 2d$, every vertex of the graph falls into either $A$ or $B$. Thus the total number of vertices is $n = |A| + |B|$.
  3. 3. Distances drop by one. For any vertex $w \in A$, the distance from $u$ must satisfy $d(w,u) \le d-1$. If $d(w,u) = d$, then the distance to $v$ via $u$ would be $d+1$, which violates the maximum diameter $d$. By symmetric reasoning, for any vertex $w \in B$ we have $d(w,v) \le d-1$.
  4. 4. Comparing the two subtrees. Viewed from the rooted tree at $u$, the set $B$ is exactly the subtree branching out from $v$. Because the unique tree structure dictates that the sizes of these subtrees are uniquely determined by the degrees of their vertices up to depth $d-1$, the structural constraints enforce that $$\deg(u) = \deg(v)$$ for any adjacent pair of vertices.
  5. 5. Conclusion. Since $\deg(u) = \deg(v)$ for all pairs of adjacent vertices, and because a graph with finite diameter must be connected, the degree of every vertex in the graph must be equal. Therefore $X$ is regular.
$\square$
The tree rooted at $u$ spans $X$ exactly once. Deleting the edge $uv$ splits the vertices into $A$ (green, closer to $u$) and $B$ (blue, the subtree hanging from $v$). Every $w \in A$ satisfies $d(w,u) \le d-1$, and symmetrically for $B$; comparing the two subtrees forces $\deg(u) = \deg(v)$.
Remarks

Regularity is exactly what makes the Moore bound meaningful: if $X$ has diameter $d$, girth $2d+1$ and every vertex has degree $k$, then the tree rooted at any vertex is full up to depth $d-1$, so

$$n \;\le\; 1 + k + k(k-1) + k(k-1)^2 + \cdots + k(k-1)^{d-1}.$$

Known Moore graphs are the complete graphs $K_{k+1}$ ($d = 1$), the odd cycles $C_{2d+1}$ (degree $2$), the Petersen graph ($d = 2$, $k = 3$) and the Hoffman–Singleton graph ($d = 2$, $k = 7$). The existence of a Moore graph with $d = 2$ and $k = 57$ is a famous open problem.

Enumeration All 34 Graphs on 5 Vertices & Self-Complementary Graphs

All $34$ pairwise non-isomorphic simple graphs on $5$ vertices: $21$ connected (rows $1$–$4$ plus the first graph of row $5$) and $13$ disconnected (the remaining graphs).
Problem

Let $X = (V, E)$ be a self-complementary $r$-regular graph. Show that $r = 2k$ for some $k$, and hence deduce that $|V| = 4k + 1$. Also show that the diameter of these graphs is $2$.

Solution
  1. 1. Degree–order relation. If $X$ is $r$-regular on $n = |V|$ vertices, its complement $\overline{X}$ is $(n-1-r)$-regular. Since $X \cong \overline{X}$, regularity is preserved, so $$r = n - 1 - r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = 2r + 1.$$
  2. 2. Parity of $\binom{n}{2}$. Self-complementarity forces $|E(X)| = |E(\overline{X})| = \tfrac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}$, so $\binom{n}{2}$ must be even. Since $n = 2r+1$ is odd, $4 \mid n(n-1)$ forces $4 \mid n-1$, i.e. $$n \equiv 1 \pmod 4.$$ Writing $n = 4k + 1$ gives $r = \dfrac{n-1}{2} = 2k$, as required.
  3. 3. Diameter is $2$. Suppose $u \not\sim v$ have no common neighbour. Then $N(u) \cap N(v) = \varnothing$, so $$|N(u) \cup N(v)| = 2r = 4k.$$ But $N(u) \cup N(v) \subseteq V \setminus \{u\}$ and $|V \setminus \{u\}| = n - 1 = 4k$. Hence $V \setminus \{u\} = N(u) \cup N(v)$, which forces $v \in N(u)$ — contradicting $u \not\sim v$.
    Therefore every non-adjacent pair has a common neighbour, so every pair of vertices is at distance $\le 2$. Since $X$ is self-complementary, it is not complete (for $n \ge 2$), so some pair is non-adjacent, giving $\operatorname{diam}(X) = 2$.
$\square$
Remarks

Such graphs exist precisely for $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. The smallest examples are $C_5$ ($n = 5$, $r = 2$) and the Paley graph of order $9$ ($n = 9$, $r = 4$); the next are the Paley graph of order $13$ and the $13$-vertex self-complementary graphs.

The diameter-$2$ conclusion is characteristic of all self-complementary graphs on $n \ge 5$ vertices, not just the regular ones.

Result 16 The Smallest Asymmetric Tree

Theorem (Smallest Asymmetric Tree)

The smallest asymmetric tree has $7$ vertices. A concrete example is the tree obtained from a central vertex $c$ by attaching three paths of lengths $1$, $2$, and $3$.

Proof (Why it is asymmetric)
  1. 1. Unique degree-3 vertex. The central vertex $c$ is the unique vertex of degree $3$. Hence every automorphism of the tree must fix $c$.
  2. 2. Branches distinguished by length. The three branches attached to $c$ have lengths $1$, $2$, and $3$. Since these lengths are pairwise different, each branch must be mapped onto itself by any automorphism fixing $c$.
  3. 3. Only the identity survives. A path has only the identity and reflection as automorphisms, but a reflection would swap the two ends of the branch. The end of a branch adjacent to $c$ is not a leaf (for branches of length $\ge 2$), so such a reflection cannot fix $c$. Therefore only the identity automorphism exists, and the tree is asymmetric.
$\square$
Remarks

Every tree on at most $6$ vertices admits a nontrivial automorphism; the example above shows the bound $7$ is attained.

For comparison, the smallest asymmetric graph (not necessarily a tree) has only $6$ vertices — trees are more rigid in this sense, and $7$ is exactly the smallest order of an asymmetric tree.

The unique degree-$3$ vertex $c$ (red) is fixed by every automorphism. The three branches have pairwise distinct lengths $1$, $2$, $3$ (blue), so each branch is preserved — leaving only the identity automorphism.

Classification Trees on 7 Vertices — Degree Sequences

Examples

Four trees on $7$ vertices, distinguished by their degree sequences:

$$(6,1^6), \qquad (5,2,1^5), \qquad (4,3,1^5), \qquad (4,2,2,1^4).$$

Here $1^m$ denotes $m$ leaves. All four are pairwise non-isomorphic, since no two share the same degree sequence.

$(6,1^6)$ — star with a degree-$6$ hub.
$(5,2,1^5)$ — one branch extended to length $2$.
$(4,3,1^5)$ — a degree-$4$ centre with one degree-$3$ branch vertex (orange).
$(4,2,2,1^4)$ — two degree-$2$ branch vertices (orange), two pendant leaves.
Remarks

These four trees are among the $11$ pairwise non-isomorphic trees on $7$ vertices. They illustrate the range of degree sequences: $K_{1,6}$ has the largest possible degree, while $(4,2,2,1^4)$ has the largest number of degree-$2$ vertices.

The asymmetric tree of the previous slide — a centre with pendant paths of lengths $1,2,3$ — has degree sequence $(3,2,2,2,1,1,1)$, which is not among the four above. Its three branch lengths being pairwise distinct is precisely what kills all nontrivial automorphisms.

In general, the degree sequence of a tree determines neither its isomorphism class nor its symmetry: here, the same collection of degrees can arise from structurally different trees.

Havel–Hakimi Algorithm & Graphical Sequences

Slide 18
1  ·  Basic Definitions
Definition 1 · Degree Sequence

A degree sequence of a graph $G=(V,E)$ with $|V|=n$ is the multiset

$$\bigl(\deg(v_1),\ \deg(v_2),\ \dots,\ \deg(v_n)\bigr),$$

usually written in non-increasing order: $d_1\ge d_2\ge\dots\ge d_n\ge 0$.

Definition 2 · Graphical Sequence

A sequence $d=(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ of non-negative integers is graphical if there exists a simple graph (no loops, no multiple edges) with $n$ vertices whose degrees are exactly $d_1,d_2,\dots,d_n$.

Handshaking Lemma

If $d=(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ is graphical then $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i$ is even.

Necessary but Not Sufficient

An even sum is necessary but not sufficient. For example $(1,1,1)$ fails immediately (odd sum). But $(3,3,3)$ also fails: its sum is $9$ (odd), yet a simple graph on $3$ vertices cannot have degree $3$ (maximum degree is $2$). The Havel–Hakimi algorithm resolves all such cases systematically.

2  ·  The Havel–Hakimi Algorithm
Algorithm (Havel–Hakimi)

Given $d=(d_1,d_2,\dots,d_n)$ with $d_1\ge d_2\ge\dots\ge d_n\ge 0$:

  1. If all entries are $0$, stop: graphical.
  2. If some entry is negative, or $d_1>n-1$, stop: not graphical.
  3. Remove $d_1$ and subtract $1$ from each of the next $d_1$ entries, i.e. set $d_i \mapsto d_i-1$ for $i=2,\dots,d_1+1$.
  4. Reorder the remaining $n-1$ entries in non-increasing order.
  5. Repeat on the new sequence.
Theorem (Havel–Hakimi, 1955)

A non-increasing sequence $d=(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ of non-negative integers is graphical if and only if the reduced sequence

$$d'=(d_2-1,\ d_3-1,\ \dots,\ d_{d_1+1}-1,\ d_{d_1+2},\ \dots,\ d_n)$$

(reordered non-increasing) is graphical.

Key Idea

Attach the highest-degree vertex $v_1$ to the $d_1$ next-highest-degree vertices. If any simple graph realises $d$, then some such graph does so with $v_1$ adjacent to precisely those $d_1$ vertices. So we may assume this without loss of generality and recurse.

3  ·  Illustrated Example 1 — a Graphical Sequence
Example 1 · $(3,3,2,2,2)$

Initial: $(3,3,2,2,2)$, $n=5$.

Step 1. Remove $d_1=3$; subtract $1$ from the next $3$ entries:

$$(3,\ \underline{3},\ \underline{2},\ \underline{2},\ 2) \longrightarrow (2,\ 1,\ 1,\ 2).$$

Reorder: $(2,2,1,1)$.

Step 2. Remove $d_1=2$; subtract $1$ from the next $2$ entries:

$$(2,\ \underline{2},\ \underline{1},\ 1) \longrightarrow (1,\ 0,\ 1).$$

Reorder: $(1,1,0)$.

Step 3. Remove $d_1=1$; subtract $1$ from the next $1$ entry:

$$(1,\ \underline{1},\ 0) \longrightarrow (0,\ 0).$$

Reorder: $(0,0)$.

Step 4. All zeros $\Rightarrow$ graphical.

Realisation of $(3,3,2,2,2)$: degrees $A=3$, $B=3$, $C=2$, $D=2$, $E=2$.
4  ·  Illustrated Example 2 — a Non-Graphical Sequence
Example 2 · $(5,5,5,1,1,1)$

Initial: $(5,5,5,1,1,1)$, $n=6$. Sum $=18$ (even, so parity does not rule it out).

Step 1. Remove $d_1=5$; subtract $1$ from the next $5$ entries:

$$(5,\ \underline{5},\ \underline{5},\ \underline{1},\ \underline{1},\ \underline{1}) \longrightarrow (4,\ 4,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0).$$

Reorder: $(4,4,0,0,0)$.

Step 2. Remove $d_1=4$; subtract $1$ from the next $4$ entries:

$$(4,\ \underline{4},\ \underline{0},\ \underline{0},\ \underline{0}) \longrightarrow (3,\ -1,\ -1,\ -1).$$

A negative entry appears $\Rightarrow$ not graphical.

Why It Fails Structurally

Each of the three degree-$5$ vertices would have to be adjacent to every other vertex. Then each degree-$1$ vertex would be adjacent to all three degree-$5$ vertices, forcing degree $\ge 3$. Contradiction.

6  ·  Pseudocode
Havel–Hakimi Pseudocode 
function isGraphical(d):
    d = sort(d, descending)
    while d is not all zeros:
        if d[0] < 0 or d[0] >= len(d):
            return False
        k = d[0]
        d = d[1:]                 # drop the first element
        for i in range(k):
            d[i] = d[i] - 1
        if any(x < 0 for x in d):
            return False
        d = sort(d, descending)
    return True
Complexity

At most $n$ iterations, each with a sort costing $O(n\log n)$.

Total: $O(n^2 \log n)$.

8  ·  Key Takeaways
Remember
  1. The sum of the degrees must be even (Handshaking Lemma).
  2. Every entry must satisfy $0 \le d_i \le n-1$.
  3. Havel–Hakimi: remove the largest entry, subtract $1$ from the next-largest entries, re-sort, and repeat.
  4. A negative entry at any stage $\Rightarrow$ not graphical.
  5. All zeros reached $\Rightarrow$ graphical.
  6. The algorithm implicitly constructs a realisation: the removed vertex is adjacent to exactly the vertices whose degrees were decremented.
